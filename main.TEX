\begin{abstract}
Corpora underpin empirical work in computational linguistics, yet the corpus
itself is rarely subjected to the systematic verification and validation (V\&V)
that comparable engineering disciplines require. We present a framework that
makes corpus V\&V operational and measurable. First, we recast the
verification/validation distinction as a falsifiable prediction about which
measurement instrument detects which class of defect, rather than as a
descriptive taxonomy. Second, we introduce Reciprocal Good--Turing (RGT)
smoothing, a parameter-free estimator for divergence between two corpora: the
reserved probability mass is set by the Good--Turing singleton estimate and
redistributed over unseen n-grams in proportion to the comparison partner's
distribution. RGT removes the free smoothing parameter and self-adapts across
n-gram orders and across languages, requiring no user-selected corpus, language, 
or n-gram-specific smoothing parameter. We use ‘parameter-free’ to mean that no user-selected smoothing parameter such as $k$ is required; the smoothing mass is determined from the observed corpus, with fixed boundary rules for degenerate cases. Third, we evaluate detection against
controlled ground truth on \emph{six} corpora spanning five languages and two
scripts; including the King James Bible, Spanish, Arabic, Igbo, and Nigerian
Pidgin, injecting five defect classes at known rates, for 2{,}400 corpus-level
and 360 document-level measurements. Across every language, RGT is monotonic in
defect rate for content defects and yields large within-corpus effect sizes
(mean $d=8.3$ for encoding corruption, $d=5.3$ for foreign-text contamination),
whereas a frequency-profile baseline representative of established practice moves
in the \emph{wrong direction} under light encoding corruption in all six corpora
($d$ from $-2.0$ to $-9.3$). The framework's central prediction holds in every
language: structural defects are weak at the corpus level (mean $d\le0.8$) but
are detected perfectly at the document level (ROC-AUC $=1.000\pm0.000$ in all
six), while content defects produce substantially larger corpus-level effects, 
a separation of at least $6.7\times$ in effect size.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Empirical computational linguistics rests on corpora, but the corpus is seldom
treated as an engineered artifact whose fitness for purpose must be
demonstrated. Kilgarriff's observation that corpus linguistics harbours a
methodological void at its centre \cite{kilgarriff2001} remains apposite: the
object at the heart of the research is the object least often subjected to
quality control.

Recent large-scale audits have made the cost of this omission concrete. A manual
audit of 205 language-specific corpora drawn from five major public multilingual
datasets found that at least fifteen contained no usable text at all, that a
substantial fraction consisted of less than half acceptable-quality sentences 
and that corpora scores were mislabelled or carried nonstandard language
codes \cite{kreutzer2022}. Duplication in training corpora has separately been
shown to degrade downstream language models \cite{lee2022dedup}. These are not
exotic failures; they are precisely the defects that a routine quality-management
process would detect.

Software and system engineering formalizes such a process as \emph{verification
and validation} \cite{boehm1984,ieee1012}. Verification asks whether the artifact
was built correctly against its specification; validation asks whether the right
artifact was built for the user's needs. Applied to corpora, verification
concerns structure: empty or truncated documents, duplicates, inconsistent
markup, mismatched document counts across a parallel pair; while validation
concerns content: whether the text is in the language claimed, whether it is
representative, whether annotations are correct.

Making this operational requires answering two questions that the literature
leaves open. \emph{Which instrument detects which defect?} A taxonomy that
classifies defects but does not tell a practitioner what to measure is of limited
use. \emph{And how do we know a proposed measure detects anything?} A measure is
commonly demonstrated by computing it between two well-known corpora, obtaining a
small number, computing it between two dissimilar corpora, obtaining a larger
number, and observing that the ordering is intuitive. This establishes that the
measure ranks obviously different texts above obviously similar ones. It does not
establish detection, because no defect was present to detect and no correct
answer was available for comparison.

We address both questions. For the second we construct ground truth by
\emph{controlled defect injection}: starting from clean corpora, we inject
defects of known type at known rates, so the correct answer is known by
construction. This enables dose–response analysis, effect-size estimation, statistical testing, and comparison with a baseline.

Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An operational V\&V framework} that maps each defect class to the
        instrument that detects it, stated as a falsifiable prediction and
        supported experimentally (Sections~\ref{sec:framework}
        and~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{Reciprocal Good--Turing smoothing}, an estimator requiring no user-selected 
        corpus-, language-, or n-gram-specific smoothing parameter, 
        for corpus divergence that derives its smoothing mass from the data and
        redistributes it using the comparison partner rather than uniformly
        (Section~\ref{sec:rgt}). It removes a free parameter whose choice changes the measured encoding-corruption effect size by 2.5× over the tested add-$k$ range.
  \item \textbf{A controlled defect-injection benchmark} for corpus V\&V,
        comprising 2{,}400 corpus-level and 360 document-level measurements over
        five defect classes and eight injection rates
        (Sections~\ref{sec:design}--\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Corpus comparison measures}
Kilgarriff and Rose surveyed measures for corpus similarity and homogeneity,
recommending a $\chi^2$ statistic computed over word-frequency profiles
\cite{kilgarriff1998,kilgarriff2001}. Frequency-profile comparison remains the
established informal instrument for judging whether two corpora resemble one
another, and we adopt a normalized form of it as our baseline for exactly that
reason.

\subsection{Compression, entropy, and divergence}
Compression provides a practical estimator of cross-entropy, and PPM-family
models have been used to estimate the entropy of natural language
\cite{cleary1984,teahan1996}. The connection to divergence is direct: the excess
codelength incurred when coding one corpus with a model trained on another is the
Kullback--Leibler divergence between them \cite{cover2006}. The Jensen--Shannon
divergence \cite{lin1991} provides a symmetric, bounded alternative whose square
root satisfies the triangle inequality \cite{endres2003}.

\subsection{Smoothing}
To obtain finite divergence over the union of the two vocabularies, nonzero probability estimates are required, motivating smoothing. Add-$k$ (Lidstone) and related
schemes are surveyed in \cite{chen1999}. Good--Turing estimation
\cite{good1953,gale1995} instead derives the mass reserved for unseen events from
the frequency-of-frequencies of the observed sample. In information retrieval,
document language models are commonly smoothed toward a background collection
model \cite{zhai2004}. Our estimator (Section~\ref{sec:rgt}) combines the
Good--Turing mass estimate with a redistribution conditioned on the comparison
partner; Section~\ref{sec:rgt-novelty} states the relationship precisely.

\subsection{Corpus documentation and auditing}
Datasheets \cite{gebru2021} and data statements \cite{bender2018} standardize
what should be \emph{recorded} about a dataset; audits of C4 \cite{dodge2021} and
of web-crawled multilingual corpora \cite{kreutzer2022} report what is actually
\emph{found} in them. These are complementary to our concern: they prescribe
documentation and report manual findings, whereas we ask which automatic
measurement reliably detects a given defect and demonstrate it against ground
truth across languages.

\section{An Operational V\&V Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

We take verification to concern the \emph{structure} of a corpus and validation
its \emph{content}. The substantive claim is that this is not bookkeeping but a
statement about where the evidence for a defect resides.

A structural defect perturbs the arrangement of documents while leaving the
aggregate distribution of linguistic material largely intact. Replacing one
document with a copy of another changes no token's corpus-wide frequency by more
than a small amount; truncating a few documents removes tokens roughly in
proportion to their existing frequencies. The aggregate n-gram distribution
barely moves, and the evidence lives in relations \emph{between} documents.

A content defect changes the linguistic material itself. Substituting a
foreign-language document introduces character sequences absent from the
reference; corrupting an encoding introduces symbols outside the script. The
aggregate distribution moves, and comparison against a trusted reference is
informative.

This yields Table~\ref{tab:framework} and the following prediction, which
Section~\ref{sec:results} tests directly in every language:

\begin{quote}
\emph{The reported experiments support the framework's prediction that corpus-level divergence is sensitive to content defects while document-level structural detectors identify duplication and truncation across all six corpora.}
\end{quote}

A further consequence is that the n-gram \emph{type} is not a free choice.
Some defects operate below the word level. Script substitution and mojibake can corrupt the character sequence itself, making word-level statistics unreliable because those statistics presuppose successful tokenization. We use word unigrams for structural defects and character trigrams for
content defects.

\section{A Measure for Corpus Comparison}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Requirements}
The task imposes four requirements: \textbf{(R1) symmetry}, since neither corpus
is privileged; \textbf{(R2) boundedness}, so magnitudes are interpretable across
corpus pairs of different sizes; \textbf{(R3) definition over the union of the two
vocabularies}, because n-grams present in one corpus and absent from the other
are among the most diagnostic evidence and must not be discarded; and
\textbf{(R4) freedom from arbitrary tuning}, since a quality-control instrument
whose verdict depends on an analyst's parameter choice is of limited evidential
value.

\subsection{Divergence}
We use the Jensen--Shannon divergence \cite{lin1991} between the n-gram
distributions $P$ and $Q$ of the two corpora: $M$ is the average distribution of $P$ and $Q$, Kullback–Leibler divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| M)$ measures how
different $P$ is from $M$;
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathrm{JSD}(P,Q) &= \tfrac{1}{2} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| M)
                   + \tfrac{1}{2} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \| M), \\
                 M &= \tfrac{1}{2}(P+Q),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
which satisfies R1 and R2: it is symmetric and bounded, $0 \le \mathrm{JSD} \le
1$ bit, equalling zero exactly when $P=Q$. It retains the compression
interpretation, since $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)$ is the expected excess codelength
in bits per n-gram incurred by coding one corpus with a model trained on the
other \cite{cover2006}. Requirements R3 and R4 concern how $P$ and $Q$ are
estimated, which is where the difficulty lies.

\subsection{Reciprocal Good--Turing smoothing}
\label{sec:rgt}

Because $\log 0$ is undefined, a divergence over the vocabulary union requires
every n-gram to receive nonzero probability under both corpora. Add-$k$ smoothing
supplies this but has two properties unsuitable here. It requires choosing $k$,
for which this setting offers no principled value (violating R4). And it
distributes the reserved mass \emph{uniformly}: an n-gram absent from corpus $A$
but frequent in $B$ receives the same probability under $A$ as one absent from
$A$ and vanishingly rare in $B$, although the two carry very different evidential
weight. A further difficulty is that the effective smoothing strength of add-$k$
is $k|V|/(N+k|V|)$, which depends on vocabulary size; since $|V|$ differs by
orders of magnitude between word unigrams and character trigrams, and between
languages, a single $k$ imposes materially different smoothing across conditions.

We therefore estimate the reserved mass from the data and redistribute it using
the comparison partner. Let corpora $A$ and $B$ have counts $C_A, C_B$, token
totals $N_A, N_B$, vocabularies $V_A, V_B$, and union $V = V_A \cup V_B$.

\textbf{Mass.} Reserve for $A$ the Good--Turing estimate of the probability that
the next token belongs to a previously unseen type \cite{good1953,gale1995},
\begin{equation}
\theta_A = n_1(A)/N_A ,
\end{equation}
where $n_1(A)$ is the number of types occurring exactly once in $A$. This is a
property of the corpus, not a tuning choice.

\textbf{Redistribution.} Let $U_A = V \setminus V_A$ be the n-grams $B$ has and
$A$ lacks. Distribute $A$'s reserved mass over $U_A$ in proportion to the
partner's distribution there and symmetrically for $B$:
\begin{equation}
P_A(g) =
\begin{cases}
(1-\theta_A)\,\dfrac{C_A(g)}{N_A}, & g \in V_A,\\[2ex]
\theta_A\,\dfrac{C_B(g)/N_B}{\sum_{g' \in U_A} C_B(g')/N_B}, & g \in U_A,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
If $U_A = \varnothing$, no unseen support exists on which to redistribute the reserved mass, and the implementation therefore uses the unsmoothed empirical distribution $P_A(g) = C_A(g)/N_A$.

\vspace{1em}Both are proper distributions over $V$, since the two
branches contribute $(1-\theta)$ and $\theta$ respectively. We floor $\theta$ at
$1/(N+1)$ to keep probabilities strictly positive when a corpus has no
singletons, and cap it at $0.5$ so observed events always retain the majority of
the mass.

The construction has a natural reading. The null model is that the two corpora
are samples of the same process; where $A$ provides no evidence, the
least-committal assumption is that $A$ resembles $B$. The divergence then measures
departure from that null rather than an artifact of where mass was arbitrarily
placed.


\subsection{Relationship to prior work}
\label{sec:rgt-novelty}
Good--Turing estimation \cite{good1953,gale1995} and smoothing toward a
background distribution \cite{zhai2004} are both long established. The
contribution is their composition for the two-corpus divergence setting: using
the Good--Turing mass estimate \emph{and} conditioning its redistribution on the
comparison partner rather than on a fixed background or a uniform prior. This
removes the free parameter, makes effective smoothing self-adapting across n-gram
orders and languages, and; as Section~\ref{sec:res-smoothing} shows, matches
the better part of the add-$k$ range with no parameter set.

\subsection{Properties}
\label{sec:props}
We verified the following empirically. Over all pairs and triples drawn from the
six corpora of Table~\ref{tab:corpora}, RGT is symmetric and satisfies the
identity of indiscernibles exactly, and all divergences lie in $[0,1]$.

Metricity requires a caveat we state explicitly. Under fixed smoothing,
$\sqrt{\mathrm{JSD}}$ is a true metric \cite{endres2003}. Under RGT the estimated
distributions are \emph{pair-dependent}: the $P_A$ used for $d(A,B)$ differs from
that used for $d(A,C)$, so the guarantee does not transfer. Empirically
$\sqrt{\mathrm{JSD}}$ satisfied the triangle inequality in all triples formed from
our corpora and in $9{,}000$ triples of corpora sampled at sizes from $20$ to
$20{,}000$ tokens; a directed search produces violations only when every corpus
in a triple is a handful of tokens in which nearly all types are singletons,
driving $\theta$ to its cap. On the corpora studied here $\theta$ ranges from
$0.005$ to $0.18$ (Table~\ref{tab:corpora}), far from that regime. Where formal
metricity is required, fixed-$k$ smoothing should be preferred; for the detection 
task studied here, RGT provides larger or comparable effect sizes without a 
user-selected smoothing parameter..

\subsection{Baseline}
As a baseline representing established practice we use the mean absolute
difference of relative frequencies over $V$:
\begin{equation}
B(C_1,C_2) = \frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{g \in V}
  \left| \frac{C_1(g)}{N_1} - \frac{C_2(g)}{N_2} \right| .
\end{equation}

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Corpus substrate}
We use six public-domain or openly-licensed corpora spanning five languages and
two scripts (Table~\ref{tab:corpora}). \textbf{English} concatenates six public-domain
novels. \textbf{Bible} is the King James Version, an archaic English register.
\textbf{Spanish} is \emph{Don Quijote}. \textbf{Arabic} is the Qur'\={a}n, our
non-Latin (Arabic-script) condition. \textbf{Igbo} and \textbf{Nigerian Pidgin}
are drawn from the AfriBERTa corpus \cite{ogueji2021}, predominantly BBC News;
Igbo is a Niger--Congo language written in Latin script with sub-dot and tonal
diacritics, and Nigerian Pidgin is an English-lexified creole. Pidgin is included
deliberately as a \emph{same-script, related-lexicon} stress case for foreign-text
detection. Project Gutenberg licence boilerplate was stripped and its absence
verified. The corpora span a wide range of the smoothing statistic $\theta$
(0.005--0.18), exercising the smoothing estimator across conditions.

\subsection{Protocol}
For each randomization seed, the corpus documents were independently shuffled 
and divided into trusted \textsc{reference} and \textsc{test} halves; the same 
seed-specific split was retained across all injection rates for that randomization. 
Because both halves originate in the same corpus, their divergence at zero injection 
provides the null distribution \emph{for that corpus}. A defect is injected into the
\textsc{test} half at rate $r \in \{0,.05,.10,.15,.20,.30,.40,.50\}$, and we
record divergence under RGT, add-$k$, and the frequency baseline. Because
injection is programmatic, the correct answer is known by construction.

Five defect classes are implemented: \textbf{duplicate} (documents replaced by
copies of others), \textbf{truncate} (documents cut to five tokens),
\textbf{boilerplate} (a repeated promotional string appended), \textbf{foreign}
(documents replaced by documents from another corpus), and \textbf{encoding}
(for encoding corruption, a fraction $r$ of TEST documents was selected, and each non-whitespace character within a selected document was independently replaced with probability 0.15 by a symbol drawn from a fixed set of mojibake-like characters). For foreign contamination each corpus
is contaminated by a script-different partner where possible; the exception is
Pidgin, contaminated with English (same script, shared lexicon) as the hardest
case.

Six corpora $\times$ 10 seeds give $n=60$ independent randomizations conditional
on the corpus per (defect, rate) cell; with five defects and eight rates this 
yields 2{,}400 corpus-level measurements, plus 360 document-level runs.

\subsection{Reporting effect sizes: within corpus, not pooled}
\label{sec:pooling}
Effect sizes must be computed \emph{within} each corpus and then averaged, not by
pooling measurements across corpora. Because corpora differ in their baseline ($r=0$) 
divergence, pooling inflates the standard deviation and attenuates the effect size. 
For foreign contamination, for instance, the naive pooled effect size at $r=0.05$ 
is $d=0.74$, whereas every individual corpus shows $d$ between $3.5$ and $7.6$. 
We therefore report within-corpus effect sizes throughout.

\subsection{Document-level detectors}
For structural defects we score each document for suspicion. Duplication is
scored as $1-\min_{j\neq i}\sqrt{\mathrm{JSD}}(d_i,d_j)$, so an exact copy scores
$1$; ground truth labels \emph{both} members of a duplicate pair. Truncation is
scored by inverse length. We report ROC-AUC against the injected labels.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Smoothing}
\label{sec:res-smoothing}
The smoothing statistic $\theta$ is self-adapting (Table~\ref{tab:corpora}):
across corpora it ranges from $0.005$ (Bible, lexically repetitive) to $0.18$
(Arabic, which has many singleton types in this corpus), and within a corpus it
falls by roughly two orders of magnitude from word unigrams to character trigrams.
A single fixed $k$ cannot reproduce this data-dependent variation across corpora 
and n-gram representations.

The choice of $k$ is consequential. Fig.~\ref{fig:smoothing} sweeps $k$ over three
orders of magnitude at the hardest operating point ($r=0.05$), reporting the
within-corpus effect size averaged over the six corpora. For encoding corruption,
it ranges from $4.6$ at $k=5$ to $11.6$ at $k=0.001$, a $2.5\times$ difference
driven entirely by a parameter choice; RGT attains $8.3$, within the strong part
of that range. For foreign contamination add-$k$ ranges from $4.8$ to $6.7$ and
RGT attains $5.3$. RGT thus matches good fixed settings without any choice being
made, and it does so simultaneously for both defect types and all six languages.

\subsection{Dose--response and the content/structural split}

Fig.~\ref{fig:dose} shows mean divergence against injection rate, averaged over
the six corpora. Content defects (foreign, encoding) rise steeply and separate
cleanly from the $r=0$ baseline distribution; the three structural defects rise 
only shallowly, as Section~\ref{sec:framework} predicts. Table~\ref{tab:effectsizes} 
gives the within-corpus effect sizes at $r=0.05$: content defects reach mean $d=8.3$ and
$5.3$, structural defects at most $0.8$, a separation of at least $6.7\times$.

To get the effect sizes, population standard deviation is used:
\[
d_{c,r} = \frac{\bar{D}_{c,r} - \bar{D}_{c,0}}{\sqrt{(s^2_{c,r} + s^2_{c,0}) / 2}},
\]
\noindent where $D_{c,r}$ denotes the divergence values for corpus $c$ at injection rate $r$, and $D_{c,0}$ denotes the corresponding zero-injection values. The reported table values are the arithmetic mean of \(d_{c,r}\) over the six corpora.

\subsection{The frequency baseline moves the wrong way, in every language}
Table~\ref{tab:encoding} contrasts RGT with the frequency baseline on encoding
corruption, per corpus, at $r=0.05$. RGT detects the defect strongly in all six
languages ($d$ from $4.6$ to $13.9$). The baseline has a \emph{negative} effect
size in all six ($-2.0$ to $-9.3$): under light corruption it reports a more
corrupted corpus as more similar to its clean reference. It is non-monotonic in
every language. Fig.~\ref{fig:encoding} shows the divergence: RGT rises
monotonically in each language while the baseline does not.

The mechanism is instructive. Mojibake substitution alters the corrupted profile 
by replacing frequent characters with symbols that were rare or absent in the clean corpus. 
This can move the corrupted profile closer to the reference under an unweighted mean-absolute-frequency criterion, even while the information-theoretic divergence increases.
The corrupted test distribution is being altered toward a flatter character-frequency distribution, while the clean reference remains unchanged. Depending on the baseline's averaging over the vocabulary, this can reduce the mean absolute frequency difference even though the test distribution has become less faithful to the reference.Whether a real defect is detected or
concealed thus depends on the choice between a frequency heuristic and an
information-theoretic quantity, and this holds across languages and scripts.

\subsection{The framework's prediction holds in every language}
The structural defects of Table~\ref{tab:effectsizes} are weak at the corpus
level (mean $d\le0.8$). Moved to the document level, different instruments detect the same defects
without error in \emph{every} corpus (corpus-level: JSD, document-level duplication: nearest-neighbour similarity, document-level truncation: inverse length): ROC-AUC = 1.000 ± 0.000 for both duplication and truncation at every non-zero injection rate tested. The verification/validation distinction thus makes a correct, falsifiable prediction about 
which instrument succeeds, and it does so language-independently within our experiment, converting a descriptive taxonomy into an operational one.

\section{Threats to Validity}

Absolute divergence values differ across corpora and genres, so we avoid absolute
interpretive thresholds and rely on the within-corpus null the injection protocol
supplies. Effect sizes are reported within corpus for the reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:pooling}.

The truncation condition is deliberately severe, reducing affected documents to five tokens; consequently, the resulting document-level performance establishes detection under extreme truncation rather than sensitivity to minimally truncated documents.

Injected defects are synthetic. They are drawn from classes reported in real
audits \cite{kreutzer2022,dodge2021}, but real corpora may exhibit correlated or
subtler defects. Synthetic injection buys a known answer at the cost of realism;
we consider this the correct trade for establishing detection, and a precondition
for studies on naturally occurring defects.

RGT trades formal metricity for adaptivity, as quantified in
Section~\ref{sec:props}. For detection this is immaterial, but applications
requiring the triangle inequality should use fixed smoothing. Finally, divergence
from a reference is not the same as poor quality: a corpus in a new genre
\emph{should} diverge from an established reference. Our protocol sidesteps this
by comparing a corpus against a clean sample of itself.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We have treated corpus verification and validation as an engineering process with
measurable outcomes, and supplied three components: a framework that maps defect
classes to detection instruments; a divergence estimator requiring no user-selected corpus, language, or n-gram-specific smoothing parameter; and a
multilingual controlled ground truth against which detection is demonstrated
rather than illustrated. Reciprocal Good--Turing smoothing removes a free
parameter whose choice alters measured effect size more than twofold, and
self-adapts across n-gram orders and across six corpora spanning five languages.
The measure is monotonic in defect rate for content defects in every language,
whereas a frequency-profile baseline moves the wrong way under light encoding
corruption in all six. The verification/validation distinction correctly predicts
which instrument detects which defect, content defects at the corpus level,
structural defects at the document level with ROC-AUC $=1.000$, language-independently.

Two limitations frame the future work. First, our substrate, while spanning two
scripts (Latin and Arabic), is confined to \emph{space-delimited} writing
systems, in which whitespace marks word boundaries. The word-level track
therefore does not yet apply to scripts that require prior segmentation; Chinese,
Japanese, and Thai, or logographic systems whose very large character
inventories would also change the character-level smoothing dynamics; extending
the framework to these is the natural next step. Second, the injected defects are
synthetic; evaluating the estimator against corpora carrying naturally occurring,
human-annotated defects, and extending the injection protocol to parallel corpora
so that sentence and, paragraph-alignment pipelines can be treated the same way,
remain open.

All corpora are public or openly licensed; the estimator, injection harness,

every figure and table regenerates from them.
